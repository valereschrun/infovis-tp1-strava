Training through data: the road to the marathon

Coursework — Information Visualization (72.74) · ITBA

In 2026 I had to cancel my original goal (Cinglés du Ventoux) and redirect all of my preparation toward the Buenos Aires Marathon on September 20th. This project explores my own training data (Strava, 2019–2026) to understand how I trained, how much, and whether I'm actually ready.

Which sport did I actually go out for the most?

Out of 236 activities logged since 2019, running clearly dominates by number of sessions (139), followed by cycling (82). The rest (snowboarding, walking, skiing, hiking) are marginal in frequency — though, as we'll see later, counting sessions can be misleading: in actual time spent training, cycling and running are much closer.

Running or cycling: which one really dominated my training?

Session count only tells half the story. When you follow cumulative distance, sessions, and time spent training month by month from 2019 to 2026, a different picture appears: cycling pulls far ahead in distance (3,170 km vs. 1,285 km) and even edges out running in total hours spent training (125.5h vs. 123.3h) — despite running happening far more often. Running wins on habit and frequency; cycling wins on volume.

What kind of runner was I building through training?

Zooming in on the running sessions alone, GPS-based pace and speed-variance analysis splits them into three natural categories, each with its own purpose in marathon training: easy runs, at a steady, conversational pace, build weekly volume without accumulating fatigue; interval training, with sharp repeated speed surges, is what raises your top-end speed and running economy so that "marathon pace" eventually feels easier; and long runs exist to bank endurance kilometers — teaching the body to keep going, burn fat efficiently, and hold form once glycogen stores start running low, which is exactly what a 42 km race demands. This is where the actual marathon preparation shows up: a structured mix of session types rather than just "going for a run," with long runs building up in both frequency and distance as the race date approached.

Did my training data predict the right marathon time — and where did it go wrong?

Based on my longest training run before the race (30 km, 8 September 2026), I used the Riegel formula — a standard race-time prediction method (T2 = T1 × (D2/D1)1.06) used by runners and coaches to project a finish time on a new distance from a known performance — to estimate a marathon finish around 3h24:32, nearly 20 minutes faster than my 2024 result (3h43). The official result on race day was 3h59:15. The kilometer-by-kilometer pace tells the full story: a steady ~4:40-5:00 min/km rhythm for 33 kilometers, then a sharp spike to over 10 min/km at kilometer 34 — the moment my knee gave out and I had to stop for about two minutes before resuming, considerably slower, to the finish. The training data was right about my fitness; it couldn't predict the injury.
